Solid colors over busy prints, cool-toned neutrals or warm accent colors, and skip all-white or all-camo — that's the short version. Here's why it works.

## Why Bluebonnets Are Hard to Dress For (and the One Rule That Fixes It)

A bluebonnet field is blue-purple and green. Your outfit's job is to contrast without clashing — busy patterns and logos compete with the flowers for attention, and exact matching across a family tends to look stiff. One dominant color per person, plus shared neutrals, photographs better than a uniform.

## Colors That Work (and Why)

Warm neutrals — cream, tan, soft white — read clean against blue. Mustard, rust, and blush add a pop without competing. Denim is a bluebonnet-photo staple for a reason: it's a cool neutral that doesn't fight the flowers.

## Colors and Patterns to Avoid

Bright green blends into the leaves and grass. Busy florals compete with the real flowers. Head-to-toe black can go flat in bright April sun. Large logos or text pull the eye away from the field.

## Fabric and Fit for an Outdoor Field Shoot

Flowy fabrics — skirts, dresses — move in the open field and photograph better than stiff fabric. Breathable cotton or linen suits April's 78°F average high. Skip brand-new stiff denim or shoes that will show discomfort on camera.

## Dressing Families and Groups Without Looking Matchy

Pick one shared color and let everyone else wear neutrals — mom in rust, everyone else in cream, denim, and tan. It coordinates without a uniform, and it works across ages easily.

## Footwear and Practical Field Gear

This is a real Texas field, not a studio floor — uneven ground, and watch your footing. Closed-toe shoes or boots are worth it. Bring a blanket or ground cloth to sit on, sunscreen, and a hat you can take off for the actual shot.
