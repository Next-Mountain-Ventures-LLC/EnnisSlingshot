This post's differentiator, and its most important section, is the honest 24-hour driver-approval step: because Ennis Slingshot requires a verification link and insurance approval that clears within roughly 24 hours (`business-context.md`), a surprise planner who books the driver's own account risks the approval email landing on the surprise recipient's phone. Write the workaround plainly (book under your own name/account as the "driver of record" if the recipient is the passenger, or give the recipient enough advance notice that the approval step reads as a normal pre-trip email, not a tell) rather than glossing over the friction.

## Structure

1. **H2: Step 1 — Pick a Date That Actually Works** — Point: surprise logistics fail most often on timing, not the gift itself. Cover: checking the partner's real calendar without asking directly, building in a buffer day in case of weather reschedule (relevant for an outdoor April activity), avoiding festival-weekend crowds if picking a specific weekend.
2. **H2: Step 2 — Decide Who Books (and Whose Name Is on the Approval Email)** — Point: this is the section most surprise-planning content skips entirely. Cover: Ennis Slingshot's booking flow (emailed verification link, ~24-hour insurance approval, only the driver needs approval); the workaround — if the recipient will be the driver, either book far enough ahead that a "pending approval" email doesn't read as unusual, or book yourself as the driver and let the recipient ride as the passenger for the surprise reveal, then let them drive on a future visit. Table: "who's driving" scenario → what to do.
3. **H2: Step 3 — Block the Calendar Without Tipping Your Hand** — Point: practical scheduling tricks. Cover: vague calendar labels, asking for the day off separately from mentioning the plan, coordinating with a friend/family member who can "need a favor" that day if cover is required.
4. **H2: Step 4 — Build a Believable Cover Story** — Point: keep it simple and low-risk. Cover: pick a cover activity in the same general direction/time window as the real plan (e.g., "lunch in Waxahachie") so a late change of plans doesn't create suspicion.
5. **H2: Step 5 — Pack for Them Without Giving It Away** — Point: logistics of physically preparing. Cover: what to have ready for an open-cockpit April drive (sunglasses, closed-toe shoes, a phone mount if they'll want photos, sunscreen) without the items themselves tipping off the surprise; where to stash a bag if they'll notice you packing.
6. **H2: Step 6 — The Reveal and the Reschedule Backup Plan** — Point: what happens on the day. Cover: arriving at the Ennis Welcome Center, the 15-minutes-early arrival window, what the free-rescheduling policy means if weather forces a change (a surprise date with a built-in "we'll go next weekend instead" backup removes most of the stress).
7. **H2: Worked Example — Surprising a Partner With an April Slingshot Ride** — Point: tie every step together in one real timeline. Cover: a sample month-by-month plan starting with a gift card purchased in December, through the April booking and reveal.
8. **H2: If You're Buying It as a Gift Instead of Planning the Date Yourself** — Point: bridge to the gift-card product. Cover: buying a gift card removes most of the booking-logistics problem (the recipient books their own date and handles their own approval), which is often the simpler surprise route; note redemption is any April 2027 day.
